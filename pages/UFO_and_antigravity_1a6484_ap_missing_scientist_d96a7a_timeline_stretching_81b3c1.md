## Introduction

One of the strongest critiques made in reporting on the alleged "missing scientists" pattern is that the [timeline]({{ 'timeline/' | relative_url }}) itself became increasingly elastic. Rather than describing a cluster of incidents occurring within a narrow period, online lists gradually expanded backwards to include deaths and disappearances stretching over several years. That change matters because a sequence spread across multiple years is statistically more likely to combine unrelated events involving people who happened to work in large scientific or defence organisations than to reveal a single coordinated campaign. Major news investigations found no verified evidence that the cases formed one connected operation, despite the growing narrative that they represented a unified pattern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cbsnews.com/news/deaths-disappearances-scientists-staff-government-labs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cbsnews.com">[cbsnews.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cbsnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CBS NewsFBI investigating deaths and disappearances of staff at...5 days ago — The disappearances and deaths of 10 government workers ti...</span></span></span>

## How the list reached back to 2022

Early online discussions focused on a handful of recent disappearances, particularly the 2026 disappearance of retired Air Force Major General William Neil McCasland. As the story spread, additional names were added from progressively earlier years. By the time the narrative reached national political attention, some versions of [the list]({{ 'the-list/' | relative_url }}) included deaths and disappearances dating back to 2022 or 2023 alongside much more recent cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missing_scientists_conspiracy_theory" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Missing scientists conspiracy theory</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Missing scientists conspiracy theory</span></span></span>

This chronological stretching changed the character of the claim. Instead of arguing that several scientists had vanished within weeks or months, proponents increasingly relied on a collection of incidents occurring over roughly three to four years. CBS News noted that the cited disappearances and deaths occurred over several years and involved researchers and staff connected with different organisations, including NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory and [Los Alamos]({{ 'los-alamos/' | relative_url }}) National Laboratory. The existence of a broad time window did not itself demonstrate coordination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cbsnews.com/news/deaths-disappearances-scientists-staff-government-labs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cbsnews.com">[CBS News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cbsnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CBS NewsFBI investigating deaths and disappearances of staff at...5 days ago — The disappearances and deaths of 10 government workers ti...</span></span></span>

The Associated Press similarly reported that the online narrative evolved from discussion of individual cases into a larger list that mixed people from different [institutions]({{ 'institutions/' | relative_url }}), different occupations and different circumstances without public evidence demonstrating a common cause.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missing_scientists_conspiracy_theory" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Missing scientists conspiracy theory</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Missing scientists conspiracy theory</span></span></span>

## Why long windows create false clusters

Human beings naturally look for patterns, especially when events involve sensitive occupations such as [aerospace]({{ 'aerospace/' | relative_url }}), nuclear research or defence work. Extending a timeline makes that tendency stronger.

Several mechanisms contribute to this effect:

* **Large populations produce rare events.** National laboratories, aerospace contractors, universities and defence agencies collectively employ many thousands of scientists, engineers, administrators and contractors. Across several years, some employees will unfortunately die unexpectedly, become victims of crime, disappear, or suffer medical emergencies simply through normal population statistics.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* **Selection bias accumulates.** Once a narrative exists, researchers and social media users often search backwards for additional cases that appear similar while overlooking the much larger number of people whose careers ended normally.
* **Different types of incidents become blended.** The published lists combined disappearances, confirmed homicides, suspected suicides, unexplained deaths and cases with known medical explanations. Treating fundamentally different events as one category makes a pattern appear stronger than it actually is.
* **Institutional association substitutes for causal evidence.** Merely sharing an employer or broad research field does not establish that separate incidents share a perpetrator or motive.

</div>

Experts interviewed by multiple outlets argued that these are classic ingredients for perceived but unproven clustering. Reporting consistently noted that investigators had not established operational links among the individual cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missing_scientists_conspiracy_theory" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Missing scientists conspiracy theory</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Missing scientists conspiracy theory</span></span></span>

## What a convincing pattern would require

A genuine coordinated campaign would normally produce evidence stronger than a retrospective collection of events spread across several years.

Investigators would typically look for factors such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* common suspects or organisations;
* shared methods of attack;
* documented communications linking victims;
* overlapping classified projects with demonstrated relevance;
* forensic evidence connecting separate investigations;
* intelligence indicating a coordinated operation.

</div>

The timeline alone cannot supply that evidence. Even if every individual case remains important and deserving of investigation on its own merits, chronological proximity is only one element of pattern analysis.

This distinction explains why congressional correspondence in April 2026 repeatedly referred to the underlying public reporting as "unconfirmed" while requesting information from federal agencies rather than asserting that a conspiracy had already been established.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://oversight.house.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/DOE-Missing-Scientists-Letter_4.20.26.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oversight.house.gov">[Oversight Committee]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oversight.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 19, 2026 — 20 Apr 2026 — Dear Secretary Wright: The Committee on Oversight and Government Reform is investigating recent unconfirme...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 19, 2026</span></span></span>

## Why the broader timeline weakened, rather than strengthened, the claim

Counterintuitively, adding more years did not necessarily make the alleged pattern more persuasive.

A narrowly defined series—for example, numerous specialists from the same programme disappearing within a few weeks under similar circumstances—would generally represent a more unusual statistical signal. Expanding the list to cover several years instead increases the opportunity to collect unrelated tragedies that only appear connected because they are viewed retrospectively.

This is one reason journalists examining the cases repeatedly distinguished between the reality of individual investigations and the claim that they formed a single campaign. Some disappearances remain unresolved, while some deaths have known or partly understood circumstances. However, combining them into one timeline does not by itself demonstrate common causation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cbsnews.com/news/deaths-disappearances-scientists-staff-government-labs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cbsnews.com">[cbsnews.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cbsnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CBS NewsFBI investigating deaths and disappearances of staff at...5 days ago — The disappearances and deaths of 10 government workers ti...</span></span></span>

The longer chronology also blurred important differences between the individuals themselves. Lists frequently mixed scientists, engineers, military officers, laboratory administrators and other personnel whose roles, employers and research programmes varied substantially. As the timeframe expanded, the common thread increasingly became only a broad association with science, defence or aerospace rather than documented participation in the same project or evidence of the same threat.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cbsnews.com/news/deaths-disappearances-scientists-staff-government-labs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cbsnews.com">[CBS News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cbsnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CBS NewsFBI investigating deaths and disappearances of staff at...5 days ago — The disappearances and deaths of 10 government workers ti...</span></span></span>

## The key lesson from the timeline critique

The criticism is not that every case has been fully explained. Several investigations have remained open or unresolved, and each deserves independent examination.

Rather, the timeline critique argues that stretching the list back to 2022 made the central conspiracy claim less, not more, compelling. A multi-year collection of heterogeneous events occurring across different institutions, under different circumstances and without demonstrated operational links is more consistent with unrelated incidents assembled into a retrospective pattern than with evidence of a single coordinated campaign.
